# Think Before You Post: Being a Safe Digital Citizen

A message, photo or comment can travel far beyond its intended audience. In Aotearoa New Zealand, the Harmful Digital Communications Act 2015 (HDCA) helps people seek support when digital communications cause serious harm. It sets standards for online behaviour and provides a process for addressing harmful communications. The Act matters because online abuse can affect a person’s safety, wellbeing and reputation, sometimes long after a post is shared.

## What can count as harmful?

Examples may include repeatedly sending threatening or abusive messages, sharing someone’s private information or intimate images without consent, posting humiliating material, spreading false allegations, or encouraging someone to harm themselves. The Act’s communication principles also cover things such as harassment, grossly offensive content, and messages that incite others to target a person. Context matters: a single unkind comment is not automatically a crime. The law’s criminal offence has specific requirements, including intent to cause harm, causing harm, and the communication being likely to cause harm to an ordinary reasonable person in the victim’s position.

Depending on the circumstances, someone affected can contact Netsafe, the approved agency, for help. A court may make orders such as requiring content to be taken down, stopping further communication, or requiring a correction or apology. Knowingly failing to comply with a court order can have consequences. A person convicted of the criminal offence of causing harm by posting a digital communication may face up to two years’ imprisonment or a fine of up to $50,000; a company may face a fine of up to $200,000. These are maximum penalties, not automatic outcomes. If you are affected, save evidence and seek advice rather than retaliating.

## Five ways to be a safe and respectful digital citizen

1. Pause before posting. Ask whether your words are true, necessary and kind.
2. Protect privacy. Get permission before sharing someone’s photo, message or personal details.
3. Keep accounts secure. Use strong, unique passwords and privacy settings.
4. Don’t join a pile-on. Avoid forwarding hurtful content; report it and support the person targeted.
5. Disagree respectfully. Criticise ideas without threatening, insulting or exposing people.

## My reflection

One thing I could do to improve my online behaviour is pause before replying when I feel annoyed. Taking a moment to reread my message could help me choose words that solve the problem instead of making it worse. Being a good digital citizen is not about never making mistakes; it is about taking responsibility, learning, and treating people online with the same respect I would show them face to face.
